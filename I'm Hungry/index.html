How to cook your Meowple!

My Beginnings

 

There are certain foods you remember eating, and then there are certain foods that seem to remember you. I know that sounds dramatic. I know it sounds like something a person would write after spending too much time staring out of a kitchen window while rain slides slowly down the glass. But if you have ever had a Meowple, you understand that some foods do not simply enter your life. They become part of the architecture of it. They settle somewhere between memory and instinct, somewhere between the smell of your childhood home and the particular sound a screen door makes when it closes behind someone you love. My story with the Meowple began long before I knew what a Meowple was. In fact, it began before I knew there was such a thing as a Meowple. I was born in Washington, in the sort of weather that teaches a child early that gray is not one color. Gray is a family of colors. There is the soft gray of a morning when the clouds sit low over the rooftops. There is the blue-gray of a November afternoon. There is the almost-purple gray that comes before a storm. And then there is the peculiar silver-gray of a wet driveway at six in the morning, when the whole world smells like cedar, cold pavement, and something cooking somewhere nearby. Those were my first landscapes. Before I understood recipes, I understood kitchens. Before I could read, I understood that certain drawers contained things adults did not want children touching. I understood that the refrigerator had a particular hum when it was working harder. I understood that the oven light could turn an otherwise dark kitchen into a tiny theater. Most of all, I understood that food had a rhythm. Breakfast had one rhythm. Sunday dinner had another. Weeknight cooking had another entirely. There were meals that happened quickly because everyone was hungry, meals that took all afternoon because someone was visiting, and meals that seemed to take place almost entirely through conversation. I grew up believing that every family had a food that belonged to them. I did not know ours was unusual. The first Meowple I ever encountered was not served to me on a plate. It was not presented at a restaurant. It was not announced with the fanfare of some grand culinary revelation. It was simply there. That is how I remember it, anyway. I was young enough that my feet did not reach the floor when I sat at the kitchen table. I remember swinging them underneath me while the rain tapped against the windows. I remember watching an adult prepare something I did not recognize. There were ingredients spread across the counter, although I could not have told you what they were. There was flour, something sweet, something buttery, and something that seemed completely out of place. And then there was the shape. The shape was unmistakable. It looked almost like a pastry, if pastries had been designed by someone who had received only a vague description of a pastry from a person who had never actually seen one. I asked what it was. The answer was: “A Meowple.” I laughed. I thought it was a joke. That was the beginning. The first bite did not immediately make sense. It was sweet, but not exactly sweet. Savory, but not exactly savory. Familiar, but also completely strange. It was the sort of taste that makes a child stop chewing for a second because their brain is trying to decide what category the food belongs in. I remember looking down at the plate. I remember looking back up. I remember asking for another one. That was my first Meowple. Or at least, that is how the story has been told. The older I get, the more I realize that childhood memories are not photographs. They are recipes. Every time we remember something, we add a little of the present to it. We adjust the proportions. We leave out certain ingredients. We emphasize others. Maybe the kitchen was smaller than I remember. Maybe the rain was not actually falling that hard. Maybe the first Meowple was not nearly as strange as I thought. But I remember the feeling. And sometimes the feeling is the most important ingredient. From that day forward, Meowples became part of my understanding of home. They appeared at odd times. They were made when someone had leftover ingredients. They were made when the weather was bad. They were made when relatives came over. They were made when there was nothing else in the house that seemed worth making. Eventually, I began asking for them. Then I began helping. And eventually, I began making them myself. I did not know then that I was learning something I would carry with me for the rest of my life. I only knew that when the kitchen smelled a certain way, I was happy.

 

My Hometown

To understand why the Meowple became so important to me, you have to understand Washington. Not the Washington that appears on postcards. Not the Washington of sweeping mountain photographs, dramatic coastlines, or carefully edited travel brochures. I mean the Washington of wet shoes. The Washington of moss. The Washington of grocery-store parking lots shining after a November rain. The Washington where you can leave the house under a completely ordinary sky and return three hours later convinced you have lived through an entire meteorological era. My hometown was the kind of place where weather was not a conversation starter because weather was the conversation. “Looks like rain.” “It was supposed to clear up.” “Did you see the forecast?” “It'll probably change.” These were not observations. They were a language. We learned to organize our lives around clouds. And in a place like that, the kitchen matters. It becomes more than a room where food is prepared. It becomes a refuge from the outside world. On rainy afternoons, I would come inside with damp hair and cold hands. The windows would be fogged. Coats would hang near the door. Shoes would collect in an untidy row. Someone would turn on the stove. And eventually, there would be Meowples. I cannot explain why the Meowple belonged so naturally to that landscape. Perhaps it was because it was warm. Perhaps it was because it was strange. Perhaps every rainy place eventually invents a food that makes no logical sense but feels emotionally inevitable. In my hometown, you could drive for miles and see enormous evergreens standing along the roads like silent witnesses. You could walk through neighborhoods where every yard seemed to contain something growing. You could smell wet bark after a storm. You could find little pockets of sunlight on days when the clouds finally broke. There was always a sense that the landscape was doing its own thing. That quality found its way into the Meowple. It was never entirely predictable. One household made them one way. Another household made them another. Someone insisted there had to be more butter. Someone else insisted there had to be less. One person swore the secret was in the filling. Another said the filling was completely irrelevant. There were arguments about texture. There were arguments about shape. There were arguments about whether a Meowple should be eaten immediately or allowed to sit for several minutes. There were, naturally, arguments about the correct pronunciation. These arguments were never resolved. They were not supposed to be. That was part of the tradition. As I grew older, I started to notice that the Meowple seemed to exist at the intersection of all the things I associated with home. It was practical but ridiculous. Comforting but confusing. Familiar but difficult to explain. You could tell someone what a Meowple tasted like, but the explanation would never quite capture it. You could show them a picture, but the picture would only raise more questions. And if you tried to explain why anyone would make one in the first place, you would quickly find yourself telling a story. That is the nature of food traditions. They are rarely logical. A recipe survives not because it is the most efficient recipe. It survives because somebody remembers somebody else making it. Eventually, I stopped thinking of the Meowple as something my family happened to eat. It became something that belonged to the place. To the rain. To the kitchens. To those long afternoons when there was nowhere else to be. And perhaps most importantly, to the peculiar Pacific Northwest ability to take something completely ordinary and make it quietly meaningful.

 

Origins

The question of where the Meowple actually came from has occupied far more of my life than I would like to admit. I have heard many explanations. I have repeated several of them. I believe almost none of them. The oldest story in my family says that the Meowple began as an accident. According to this version, someone was attempting to make a completely different dish and accidentally combined the wrong ingredients. Rather than throwing the mixture away, they cooked it. The result was supposedly the first Meowple. This explanation has the satisfying simplicity of a family legend. Unfortunately, it raises several questions. What dish were they trying to make? Who was this person? Why did they have those ingredients? Why did they give the finished product the name “Meowple”? No one knows. Another version claims the name came from a child who was asked what the dish should be called and simply made something up. This is also plausible. Children have named many things in human history. Some of those names have survived. Most have not. There is also a story that the Meowple was originally created during a particularly rainy Washington winter by someone who wanted to make something comforting from whatever happened to be available in the pantry. This version is my favorite. Not because I think it is true. I don't. I like it because it feels true. And sometimes family traditions survive on that distinction. The truth is, I have never found a definitive beginning. There is no moment in history when a person stands in a kitchen, looks down at the ingredients, and says, “Today, I shall create the Meowple.” There is no parchment. There is no founding document. There is no famous chef standing behind the inventor, nodding knowingly. There is only food. And people. And time. The first written Meowple recipe I ever encountered was almost certainly not the first recipe ever written. It was handwritten. That mattered to me. There is something different about a handwritten recipe. The handwriting reveals hesitation. It reveals confidence. It reveals which ingredients mattered enough to write down and which were apparently so obvious that they could be left to interpretation. Measurements were inconsistent. Instructions were vague. At one point, the recipe simply said: “Cook until it looks right.” I found this both charming and infuriating. What does “looks right” mean? Right according to whom? How brown is brown enough? How soft is soft enough? How much Meowple is too much Meowple? These questions have no universal answers. That is why, over the years, I developed my own relationship with the recipe. I stopped trying to discover the one true Meowple. Instead, I began thinking about the Meowple as a category. A philosophy. A loose agreement between ingredients. Something that begins with a recipe and ends with judgment. The Meowple became less about precision and more about recognition. You knew when it was ready. You knew when the smell had changed. You knew when the outside had reached that particular color. You knew because someone had shown you. And then, without realizing it, you became the person who knew. That is how food traditions work. They move quietly. One generation makes something. The next generation watches. Then the next generation changes it. And eventually someone asks where it came from. Everyone looks at everyone else. And the recipe continues.

 

Family

If the Meowple has an origin, it lives in my family. Not in a single person. Not in a single kitchen. Not in a single recipe card. It lives in the accumulated habits of people who stood around counters, opened cabinets, tasted things, disagreed with one another, and insisted that they remembered the recipe correctly. My family was never particularly good at agreeing on food. That may be why the Meowple survived. Every family has its arguments. Ours simply had unusually specific culinary arguments. There were disagreements about how much of an ingredient to use. There were disagreements about when something was finished. There were disagreements about whether a particular batch was better than the previous one. And there were disagreements about who had originally taught whom how to make it. This last argument could become surprisingly intense. One person would say they learned from Grandma. Someone else would point out that Grandma had never made them that way. Someone would insist that there was an old recipe somewhere. Someone else would say the old recipe was wrong. Then everyone would eat the Meowples. The argument would continue. This, in retrospect, was probably the most authentic part of the tradition. Food brings people together, but it also gives them something to disagree about. The kitchen becomes a courtroom. Everyone has evidence. Everyone has witnesses. Everyone is absolutely certain. And eventually someone says, “Fine. Just make them the way you remember.” So you do. That is how I learned. Not from a formal lesson. Not from a cooking school. Not from a carefully structured demonstration. I learned by standing nearby. I learned by watching hands. I learned by noticing what people did without thinking. I learned that some ingredients were measured and others were simply understood. I learned that there is a difference between following a recipe and knowing a recipe. A recipe tells you what to do. Experience tells you when to stop. When I eventually began making Meowples on my own, I discovered that I had inherited more than a recipe. I had inherited opinions. I had inherited preferences. I had inherited tiny movements of the hands. I had inherited the instinct to check something before the timer went off. I had inherited the habit of tasting. I had inherited the belief that if something did not look right, it probably wasn't right, even if the recipe said otherwise. And I had inherited the deeply inconvenient certainty that my way was the correct way. I suspect everyone else in my family felt exactly the same. Years passed. The kitchens changed. The houses changed. The people changed. Some of the old dishes disappeared. Some were replaced by newer ones. Some recipes were forgotten entirely. But the Meowple remained. Whenever I made them, I could remember those rainy Washington afternoons. I could remember the kitchen table. I could remember the sound of utensils against bowls. I could remember conversations I had forgotten until the smell brought them back. That is the strange power of food. It does not simply remind us of the past. Sometimes it gives the past somewhere to live. And for me, that place was always the Meowple.
